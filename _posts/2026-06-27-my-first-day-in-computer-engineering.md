There are moments in life that stay with you not because they were perfect, 
but because they were the beginning of something that changed you. My first 
day as a Computer Engineering student at UET Lahore's Faisalabad Campus was 
exactly that kind of moment. I walked in excited, but also deeply uncertain. 
Unlike many of my classmates who had prior experience with computers and 
programming, I was coming in with almost no background at all. When I saw the 
course outline and realized that most of my subjects were coding-based, with 
Python as a major component, my heart sank a little. I remember thinking — 
have I chosen the right field?

That question did not stay unanswered for long. On the very first day, we had 
an orientation session, and it was conducted by Dr. Bilal Ahmad. From the 
moment he started speaking, something shifted. He did not make coding sound 
like a wall we had to climb — he made it sound like a door we were about to 
open. He introduced us to Python and began teaching from the absolute basics, 
which was exactly what someone like me needed. His calm, structured approach 
made the subject feel approachable rather than terrifying, and that first 
impression mattered more than I realized at the time.

Still, the first two to three weeks were genuinely difficult. Coding felt like 
learning a completely new language — not just in terms of vocabulary, but in 
terms of how you think. A human language allows for some vagueness. A 
programming language does not. One wrong bracket, one missing colon, and 
nothing works. I struggled to understand even the simplest concepts. What is 
a variable? Why does indentation matter in Python? Why does the program do 
nothing when I run it? These were questions I was almost embarrassed to ask 
out loud, but they were very real. Dr. Bilal Ahmad's patience in answering 
them — and his ability to explain things in multiple ways until they clicked — 
was what kept me going through those early weeks.

What made everything more complex was that I was also adjusting to hostel 
life at the same time. Moving away from home, sharing a room with someone I 
had just met, managing my own schedule, cooking, laundry, sleep — all of this 
was happening simultaneously with one of the most intellectually demanding 
subjects I had ever encountered. Programming requires full concentration. It 
requires you to sit with a problem until you understand it. That kind of deep 
focus is hard to achieve when you are still figuring out how to live 
independently for the first time.

There were evenings when I sat in front of my laptop, staring at code that 
made no sense, feeling genuinely lost. But there were also mornings when 
something small clicked — when a loop finally did what I wanted it to do, or 
when a function returned the right output — and those small moments of clarity 
kept me motivated. I began to understand something important: in programming, 
progress is not always visible. You can spend hours on a problem and feel like 
you have learned nothing, and then suddenly, in the next session, three things 
make sense at once. That is just how it works.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad, beyond being an excellent teacher of Programming Fundamentals, 
is someone whose broader work in Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning 
gave our early lessons a sense of direction. Even when we were writing the 
simplest Python programs, there was always a sense that we were building toward 
something real. He works with real-world datasets — particularly medical data, 
which demands high accuracy and precision — and that commitment to meaningful, 
applied work filtered into how he taught us even at the foundational level. 
He was not just teaching us syntax. He was teaching us how to think like 
engineers.

Looking back at that first day now, I see it differently than I did when I 
was living it. At the time, it felt like the start of something I was not 
sure I could handle. In hindsight, it was the start of a process that has 
already changed how I think, how I approach problems, and how I see my own 
potential. I did not walk in as a confident programmer. I walked in as someone 
who was willing to try, and for a first day, that was enough.

This blog exists partly because of what that first day set in motion. Dr. Bilal 
Ahmad encouraged us to document our journeys — to leave a digital footprint 
that reflects not just our achievements but our growth. You can explore his 
research and professional work on his 
[LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/). For anyone 
starting their own journey in Computer Engineering, my advice is simple: show 
up, stay curious, and do not let the confusion of the first few weeks convince 
you that you do not belong. You do.
