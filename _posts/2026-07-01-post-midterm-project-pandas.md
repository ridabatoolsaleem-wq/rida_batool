After midterms, we were assigned a project that introduced us to something 
entirely new — Pandas, a Python library used for data analysis. The project 
was based on a heart attack risk prediction dataset, and we had to apply 
regression techniques to it. We were given around ten days to complete it, 
and I worked in a group of two.

The topic itself was fascinating. Heart attack risk prediction is exactly 
the kind of real-world problem that makes data science feel meaningful. 
Dr. Bilal Ahmad has always pushed us toward datasets that matter — medical 
data in particular, because it is collected with high precision and the 
stakes of getting it wrong are serious. Working on this project, even at 
a beginner level, gave me a small sense of what that kind of work actually 
involves.

However, the experience also exposed a problem I had been avoiding — my 
tendency to step back and let others take the lead. My group mate handled 
almost everything. He installed the libraries, prepared the dataset, trained 
the model, and completed the manual. I asked multiple times to be involved, 
but somehow I never pushed hard enough to actually participate. As a result, 
I did not fully understand how the project worked until the night before the 
exhibition, when my group mate finally walked me through it.

I memorized the key points quickly and prepared for the viva. On the day of 
the exhibition, Dr. Bilal Ahmad asked questions during the viva, and 
fortunately, I was able to answer them. We received good marks. From the 
outside, it looked like a success.

But I knew the truth. Good marks on a project you did not fully do yourself 
are not really yours. The knowledge was not mine, the process was not mine, 
and if someone had asked me a deeper question, I would not have had an answer. 
That realization was more valuable than the marks themselves.

This project taught me that passive participation is not participation at all. 
In group work, it is easy to let capable people carry everything — but that 
only benefits your grade, not your learning. Next time, I intend to be the 
one who installs the libraries, prepares the data, and understands every step 
of the process. That is the only way any of this actually becomes useful.

You can see the kind of applied, meaningful ML work that Dr. Bilal Ahmad 
conducts through his 
[LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/). His standard 
for real, hands-on engagement with data is exactly what I should have brought 
to this project — and what I plan to bring to the next one.
