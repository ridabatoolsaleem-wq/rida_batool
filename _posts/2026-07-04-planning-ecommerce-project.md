In our Database Systems course, we were assigned a project to build an 
e-commerce platform. The goal was to apply everything we had learned — 
tables, relationships, queries — into something that resembled a real 
business application. Working in pairs, we had to think not just like 
students completing an assignment, but like developers solving an actual 
problem.

I partnered with a classmate who ran a beauty parlor business. We decided 
to build an online platform for selling hair oil products. It felt like a 
natural fit — she had domain knowledge of the business, and I could 
contribute on the technical side. The idea was practical, grounded in a 
real business, and something we were both genuinely interested in building.

We worked on the initial plan with enthusiasm. We mapped out the product 
categories, thought about how customers would browse and purchase, and 
started thinking about the database structure behind it all. For the first 
time in a group project, I felt actively involved from the beginning — 
which was a conscious effort after what I had learned from my Pandas 
project experience earlier.

Then Dr. Bilal Ahmad reviewed our idea and advised us to pause. Many 
groups, he pointed out, were working on similar e-commerce concepts, and 
he wanted us to think more creatively. He encouraged us to come up with 
something unique — a project that stood out rather than one that blended 
in with dozens of similar submissions.

At first, being asked to change direction felt discouraging. We had already 
invested time and thought into the hair oil platform. But stepping back, 
I understood the reasoning. In real-world development, the ability to 
pivot — to recognize when an idea is not distinctive enough and to find 
a better one — is just as important as the ability to execute. Dr. Bilal 
Ahmad was not just evaluating our database skills. He was pushing us to 
think like engineers who bring something original to the table.

This redirection taught me more than any completed project could have. 
It taught me that flexibility is a skill. That creativity under constraint 
is more valuable than creativity with no boundaries. And that feedback, 
even when it interrupts your progress, is usually pointing you toward 
something better.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad's own research reflects this mindset. His work in Machine 
Learning, particularly with medical datasets, requires constant evaluation 
and adjustment — a model that is not performing well needs to be 
reconsidered, not just pushed harder. You can read more about his 
research on his 
[LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/). That same 
willingness to change direction when something is not working is what 
he was teaching us, in a different context, through this project.

We are still working on the new idea. But I am approaching it differently 
this time — with more ownership, more creativity, and a better understanding 
of what it means to build something worth building.
