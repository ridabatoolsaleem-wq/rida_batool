Looking back at this semester, I did not just study individual subjects. 
I studied a collection of ideas that, taken together, started to form a 
bigger picture of what Computer Engineering actually is. Digital Logic 
Design, Object-Oriented Programming, and Database Systems were three very 
different courses — but the lessons they taught me overlapped in ways I 
did not expect.

Digital Logic Design was probably the most abstract subject I encountered 
this semester. It dealt with the fundamental building blocks of how 
computers work at the hardware level — logic gates, truth tables, Boolean 
algebra. At first it felt completely disconnected from anything practical. 
But gradually I understood that every program I write in Python, every 
query I run in MySQL, ultimately runs on hardware that operates on exactly 
these principles. DLD taught me that there are layers to computing, and 
that understanding lower layers makes you a better engineer at every level 
above them.

Object-Oriented Programming introduced me to a completely different way of 
thinking about code. Instead of writing a sequence of instructions, OOPs 
asks you to think in terms of objects — entities that have properties and 
behaviors. It was a difficult mental shift, especially because I was still 
building confidence in basic programming. But once it started to click, I 
could see why it matters. Real software is built on these principles, and 
learning them early — even imperfectly — gave me a foundation I will build 
on in every semester that follows.

Database Systems, which Dr. Bilal Ahmad taught us, was the subject where 
everything felt most connected to the real world. Data is everywhere. Every 
application, every website, every system stores and retrieves information 
through some form of database. Learning how to design those databases — 
how to structure data so that it is organized, accessible, and reliable — 
felt immediately useful in a way that some other subjects did not.

What these three subjects taught me collectively was something beyond their 
individual content. They taught me that Computer Engineering is not one 
skill — it is many skills working together. Hardware and software. Logic 
and creativity. Structure and flexibility. No single course gives you the 
complete picture. You need all of them, and you need time to see how they 
connect.
