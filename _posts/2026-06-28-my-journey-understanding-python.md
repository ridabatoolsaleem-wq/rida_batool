Learning Python was one of the hardest things I did this semester. I came 
into Computer Engineering without any coding background, so when I first 
opened a Python file, I genuinely did not know where to begin. The syntax 
looked strange, the logic felt foreign, and even small programs confused me. 
But this is the story of how I slowly started to find my footing.

One thing that helped me early on was switching to Visual Studio Code. 
Compared to other tools, it had a cleaner interface and did not overwhelm 
me with unnecessary options. I could focus on writing code rather than 
figuring out the software itself. That small change made a noticeable 
difference in how comfortable I felt while practicing.

One of my biggest struggles was writing code on paper during quizzes. When 
you code on a computer, you get instant feedback — an error message tells 
you what went wrong. On paper, there is no feedback. You have to carry the 
entire logic in your head, remember the exact syntax, and write it correctly 
in one go. I made many mistakes in those quizzes — wrong indentation, missing 
colons, variables named inconsistently. It was frustrating, but it also forced 
me to actually memorize and understand the code rather than just run it and 
see what happens.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad helped a great deal during this period. He provided detailed 
PDFs for every topic we covered — notes that explained each concept step by 
step with examples. Those notes became my main revision resource. Whenever I 
felt stuck, I went back to them. His teaching style never made us feel 
embarrassed for not knowing something, which made it easier to ask questions 
and admit confusion.

I also had to be honest with myself about the mistakes I was making. I was 
avoiding daily practice, depending on classmates to explain things instead of 
figuring them out myself, and rushing through concepts without really 
understanding them. Once I recognized these habits, I started working against 
them — slowly, but consistently.

Python never became easy for me overnight. But by the end of the semester, 
I was in a different place than where I started. I could read code and 
understand what it was doing. I could write simple programs independently. 
And most importantly, I stopped feeling afraid of it. That shift in confidence 
was the real achievement — not the marks, but the mindset.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad, who also specializes in Machine Learning and trains models 
on real-world datasets, always reminded us that programming is a skill built 
through repetition and patience. His work often involves medical datasets 
where precision is everything — and that same standard of care, he encouraged 
us to bring to our own learning. You can follow his work and research on his 
[Facebook page](https://www.facebook.com/Dr.BilalAhm). That perspective 
kept me motivated even when progress felt slow.

If you are a beginner struggling with Python, know that the confusion you 
feel in the beginning is normal. It does not mean you cannot do it. It just 
means you have not done it enough yet.
