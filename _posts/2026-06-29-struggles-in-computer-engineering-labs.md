Lab sessions were one of the most challenging parts of my first semester. 
Each session lasted around three hours, which felt very long — especially 
for a subject like programming that requires full concentration throughout. 
I understood that practice was necessary, but sitting with code for that 
long, when I was still struggling with basics, was genuinely exhausting.

Our lab instructor, Madam Ofi, followed a structured approach. Before each 
session, she would send us a manual to read in advance. During the lab, she 
explained tasks on the board and guided us step by step. This method was 
helpful, but it also required strong focus and prior preparation — both of 
which I struggled with in the beginning. I often came underprepared, which 
made the sessions harder than they needed to be.

The lab environment added its own challenges. Electricity issues sometimes 
interrupted our work mid-session, breaking concentration and wasting time. 
These interruptions were frustrating, but they also taught me something 
useful — that in real engineering work, conditions are rarely perfect. You 
learn to keep going anyway.

One incident that stayed with me was when my incomplete manual was not 
accepted. At the time, it felt unfair. Looking back, it was one of the most 
important lessons of the semester. Incomplete work is not work. Responsibility 
means finishing what you start, not just attempting it. That single rejection 
changed how I approached every submission after that.

The evaluation method also evolved during the semester. Early on, tasks were 
checked individually, which felt stressful and exposed every gap in my 
understanding. Later, quizzes were introduced, which were easier to manage 
and gave me a clearer sense of what I actually knew. Both methods pushed me 
to improve in different ways.

By the end of the semester, I could see real progress in how I handled lab 
sessions. I was more prepared, more focused, and more comfortable with the 
process. The three hours that once felt endless started to feel productive. 
That shift did not happen because the labs got easier — it happened because 
I got more serious.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad always emphasized in his lectures that consistency is what 
separates those who improve from those who stay stuck. As someone who trains 
Machine Learning models on precise, real-world data, he understands better 
than most that results come from repeated, disciplined effort — not from 
occasional bursts of activity. That lesson applied just as much to lab 
sessions as it does to model training. For more on his work and approach, 
you can visit his 
[Google Scholar page](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en).

Labs were tough. But they were worth it.
