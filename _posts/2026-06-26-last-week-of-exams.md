If there is one thing I will remember about this semester for the rest of my degree, it is the last week of exams. Not because I performed brilliantly — quite the opposite. That week taught me one of the most uncomfortable lessons a student can learn: that there is no shortcut for preparation done in time, and that when you delay things long enough, everything collapses at once.

By the final week, I was carrying the weight of pending assignments from multiple courses simultaneously. Database Systems had submissions due. DLD, which is Digital Logic Design, had its own load. Object-Oriented Programming had quizzes stacking up. And in the middle of all of this, the actual final exams were approaching. The days blurred together. I remember going to university early in the morning, staying through the afternoon, and still feeling like I had not done enough. There were days when I barely had time to eat a proper meal. I was not managing a schedule anymore — I was just surviving one deadline at a time.

The honest truth is that this was entirely avoidable. Looking back now, with the clarity that only comes after the storm has passed, I can see exactly where things went wrong. It was not the last week that broke me — it was every earlier week where I told myself I had time. Every assignment I pushed to "tomorrow," every concept I said I would revise "later," all of it collected interest, and the final week was when the debt came due all at once.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad, who teaches us Programming Fundamentals and Database Systems, has a way of saying things in class that only make sense later. He often talks about precision and planning — not just in the context of code or databases, but as a general approach to work. He trains ML models on real-world datasets, particularly medical data, where timing and accuracy are non-negotiable. A model trained on bad data at the wrong time does not just get a low grade — it fails at something that matters. That philosophy, I now realize, applies directly to how a student should manage their semester. You cannot rush preparation. You cannot compress weeks of learning into two days and expect it to hold.

Coming through that week changed something in how I think about my time. I am not saying I have it all figured out now — I am still a first-year student who makes mistakes. But I walked out of that exam hall with a lesson that no lecture could have given me as directly: prepare early, and prepare continuously. Not in one sitting before the deadline, but steadily, across the whole semester.

The other thing that week revealed was the importance of prioritization. When everything feels urgent, nothing gets done well. I was switching between subjects so rapidly that I could not give any one of them the attention it deserved. A better approach — which I intend to follow next semester — is to distribute work as it is assigned, keep a running list of pending tasks, and treat early completion not as overachievement but as basic responsibility.

I am writing this post as an honest reflection, not to dramatize stress or make the experience sound more difficult than it was for others. Many of my classmates were equally stretched, and we all got through it. But I am writing it here, on this portfolio, because Dr. Bilal Ahmad encouraged us to document our real experiences — not just our polished victories. The point of this blog is not to present a perfect student. It is to present a real one, who is learning, sometimes the hard way, and who is willing to be honest about that.

Next semester, I will do better. Not because I have to, but because I now know what it costs not to.
