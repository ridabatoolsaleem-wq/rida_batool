Toward the end of the semester, we were given a task that felt different 
from everything we had done before. Instead of being handed a dataset and 
told what to do with it, we were asked to create our own machine learning 
dataset ideas. Each group had to come up with three to four ideas, and one 
would be selected to move forward with. It sounds simple, but it was 
actually one of the most demanding tasks of the semester — because it 
required original thinking, not just execution.

I remembered my earlier experience with the Pandas project, where I had 
let my group mate handle everything and learned almost nothing as a result. 
This time, I was determined to do things differently. I sat down, thought 
carefully about real-world problems, and came up with three dataset ideas 
on my own. Each one was based on something practical — problems that 
actually exist and where data-driven solutions could make a difference.

The process of thinking through dataset ideas is more involved than it 
sounds. You have to ask yourself what problem you are trying to solve, 
what kind of data would be needed to solve it, whether that data is 
realistically collectable, and whether the problem is specific enough to 
be meaningful. Dr. Bilal Ahmad had always emphasized working with datasets 
that have real value — particularly medical datasets, which are gathered 
with high precision because the cost of error in medicine is too high to 
ignore. That standard shaped how I approached this task.

Going through that thinking process — even at a beginner level — gave me 
a much clearer sense of how Machine Learning actually begins. It does not 
begin with a model or an algorithm. It begins with a problem and a question. 
What are we trying to predict? What data do we need? Is the data reliable? 
These are the questions that come before any code is written, and they are 
the questions that determine whether the work that follows is meaningful 
or not.

This task also rebuilt my confidence in a way that grades alone never could. 
I had contributed something original. The ideas were mine — thought through, 
written up, and presented by me. That sense of ownership over my own work 
was something I had been missing, and once I experienced it, I understood 
why it matters so much.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad's approach to ML research, which you can explore through 
his [LinkedIn profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/), is built 
on exactly this kind of intentional, problem-first thinking. He does not 
train models for the sake of training models — he identifies problems that 
have real consequences and works backward from there. Being exposed to that 
mindset, even indirectly through a semester of coursework, has changed how 
I think about what technology is actually for.

I am still a beginner. But I am a beginner who now understands that the 
most important step in any data project is the first one — choosing the 
right problem to solve.
