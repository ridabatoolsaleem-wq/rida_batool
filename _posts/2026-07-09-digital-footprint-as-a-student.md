When I first heard the phrase "digital footprint," I thought it was 
something that applied to famous people — celebrities, politicians, 
influencers. Not to a first-year Computer Engineering student who was 
still figuring out how to write a Python loop correctly. But Dr. Bilal 
Ahmad changed that thinking early in the semester, and looking back now, 
I am glad he did.

His suggestion was simple: build a portfolio website on GitHub Pages and 
use it to document your learning journey. Leave evidence that you exist 
as a student — not just in university records, but on the internet, where 
the professional world actually looks. At the time, it felt like an 
additional task on top of an already full semester. By the end of the 
semester, it felt like one of the most valuable things I did.

A digital footprint is not just about being visible. It is about being 
findable in the right way. When someone searches your name — a future 
employer, a collaborator, a graduate school — what do they find? If the 
answer is nothing, that is itself a statement. It says you have not done 
anything worth documenting, or that you are not serious enough about your 
field to engage with it publicly. Neither is a good impression to make, 
especially in a field like technology where your work can speak for itself 
if you give it a place to be heard.

This blog has been my attempt to create that place. Fifteen posts, written 
across the length of this semester, covering everything from my first 
confused day in a programming class to group projects, lab struggles, exam 
reflections, and dataset ideas. Not all of it was comfortable to write. 
Some of it required me to be honest about mistakes I would have preferred 
to forget. But that honesty is exactly what makes it real — and real is 
what makes it worth reading.

I also learned something about writing itself through this process. 
Explaining an experience forces you to understand it. When I wrote about 
the Pandas project, I had to confront the fact that I had not participated 
enough. When I wrote about the exam week, I had to admit that the stress 
was my own doing. Writing did not let me be vague about those things the
